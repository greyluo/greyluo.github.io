---
title: "The Danger of Taking Prosperity for Granted"
subtitle: "Whatever we treat as a default, we slowly stop working to protect."
date: 2026-10-06
---

I've come to believe that a lot of the risks our society faces don't come from outside. They come from having been rich for so long that we've started to take things for granted.

It's a strange side effect of prosperity. The longer something has been around, the less it feels like an achievement and the more it feels like a default. And whatever we treat as a default, we slowly stop working to protect.

## Somebody has to keep the lights on

Consider what most of us now treat as the bare minimum: clean water, stable electricity, enough food, maybe even air conditioning. We talk about these as things we deserve, things that should just be there.

But who decided that was the bare minimum? Nothing about it is natural. Somebody has to run the treatment plants, fix the pipes, keep the grid balanced, and invest decades ahead. For most of human history, none of it existed.

The danger of believing these things are supposed to be there is that the effort behind them becomes invisible. Once enough people stop seeing that effort, fewer put it in, as workers, as taxpayers, or as voters. Nothing breaks overnight. Things just get a little worse every year, until one day they're gone.

South Africa is a sobering example. It once had stable electricity and tap water you could drink. Over the past two decades it has lived through years of rolling blackouts, and in many towns the water is no longer safe straight from the tap. People there never stopped wanting power or clean water. Keeping them running just stopped being a priority.

The same goes for clean streets, working public transit, and safe parks. All of it takes ongoing effort. When a society treats these things as given, the neglect eventually shows up where it matters most: in who gets elected, and what they decide is worth funding.

## Freedom doesn't come with a warranty

Institutions follow the same pattern. In the West especially, democracy, the rule of law, and the idea of justice are taken for granted. People are supposed to be free. There's supposed to be a guardrail between right and wrong. If something goes wrong, justice will show up eventually.

Because those ideas feel permanent, every failure starts to look like proof that the whole system is broken. Something unjust happens, and the conclusion is that the system is rotten and needs to be overhauled. Rarely does anyone stop to weigh how much good that same system quietly does for almost everyone, almost every day.

That instinct is dangerous. So much of the modern world rests on these systems. They're imperfect, but they took a long time to build, and tearing them up can lead to outcomes nobody intended: crime, chaos, and the kind of instability that undermines the economy and daily life.

It's hard not to see this behind how much of today's politics is drifting toward the extremes. When you take for granted what a system gives you, burning it down starts to look cheap.

## Renovate, don't demolish

If we want a society that stays rational and keeps progressing sustainably, it starts with appreciation: of our institutions, our public services, how good modern life actually is, and how we got here at all.

That doesn't mean nothing should change. It means whatever we change should be built on the consensus we already share, not on its ruins.

This is where history earns its keep. It shows how the modern world was made, which problems each system was built to solve, and what solving them cost. Better to carry those lessons forward than relearn them the hard way.

## Blame it on Reagan?

Take a controversial one: the popular claim that Reagan ruined America through neoliberalism.

Judging that fairly means remembering that the problems back then were very different from the ones we have now. The country was stuck in stagnation. State-run and heavily regulated sectors were inefficient. The federal government was over budget and full of redundancy, and powerful unions had outsized influence over economic activity. All of that slowed the economy down.

Market reform and deregulation were a reasonable response. We still live with the benefits today. The problem is that those benefits have been taken for granted, so all anyone sees now are the downsides and inefficiencies.

If the answer to neoliberalism is simply to go back to what came before it, that's a mistake. It ignores the problems the reforms were trying to solve in the first place.

## Boring is the goal

AI is heading down the same road, and as someone who builds it, this is the part I care about most.

There's already a camp arguing that the answer to an AI-shaped world is to go back to how things were before it. That won't work. AI is becoming infrastructure, and infrastructure tends to stay.

In fact, being taken for granted is the best possible outcome for AI. The goal is a world where people think about it the way they think about electricity or water: always there, quietly working, barely noticed. Getting there would be a huge success, and it's a future worth building.

But the same trap is easy to see coming. Someday people may look at the problems AI created and decide we should just go back to the world before it. That won't be the answer either. AI will be imperfect, like every system before it, and the work will be to improve it, not rip it out.

## Mind the fence

Step back, and it's miraculous that any of this happened: clean water, reliable power, laws that protect strangers, machines that can think alongside us. It took an enormous amount of effort to get here, and that's easy to forget.

G.K. Chesterton had a test for this, now known as Chesterton's fence. A reformer finds a fence across a road and wants it gone because he can't see what it's for. The wiser reformer says that's exactly why you can't remove it yet: "Go away and think." Come back when you know why it was put there.

Not bad for a species that, not that long ago, was still figuring out fire.
